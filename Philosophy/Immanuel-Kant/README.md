### Enlightenment 
Enlightenment is man's emergence from his self-imposed nonage. Nonage is the inability to use one's own understanding without another's guidance. This nonage is self-imposed if its cause lies not in lack of understanding but in indecision and lack of courage to use one's own mind without another's guidance. Dare to know! (Sapere Aude.) "Have the courage to use your own understanding" is therefore the motto of the Enlightenment.

## Copernican turn in philosophy
Kant sets out the Copernican turn in the preface to the second edition of the _Critique of Pure Reason_ (1787).

**The problem.** Metaphysics had made little progress, while mathematics and physics had found secure footing. Kant asked why. Up to then, everyone had assumed that our knowledge must conform to objects. On that assumption, knowing anything about objects _a priori_ (with necessity, before experience) is a mystery, because you can’t know in advance what an independent thing will be like. Yet geometry and the principle that every event has a cause seem to be exactly that kind of knowledge.

**The reversal.** Kant proposed the opposite hypothesis: objects must conform to our cognition. He compares this to Copernicus, who couldn’t explain the heavens while assuming the stars revolved around the observer, so he tried letting the observer move instead. Some of what we see in the sky comes from our own standpoint and motion. In the same way, some features of every experienced object come from the structure of the mind that experiences it.

**What the mind contributes.**

- _Space and time_ are forms of intuition, the way anything must appear to us to be perceived at all. They are not properties of things as they are in themselves.
- _The categories_, such as causality, substance, and unity, are pure concepts of the understanding that organise what we perceive into a coherent world of objects.
- _The unity of apperception_, the “I think” that must be able to accompany all my representations, holds this together as one experience.

This explains synthetic a priori knowledge. We can know with certainty that every possible object of experience will be spatial, temporal, and causally ordered, because that order is the condition of anything becoming an object for us in the first place. As Kant puts it, we know a priori only what we ourselves put into things.

**The price and the payoff.**

- _Transcendental idealism._ We know things only as _appearances_ (phenomena), never as _things in themselves_ (noumena). Theoretical knowledge is limited to possible experience. Kant still holds that the world we experience is fully real and lawful, a position he calls empirical realism.
- _The limits of metaphysics._ Claims about God, the soul, and the cosmos as a whole go beyond any possible experience. Reason tangles itself in contradictions when it tries to prove them.
- _Room for freedom._ Because nature’s causal law governs only appearances, freedom isn’t ruled out at the level of things in themselves. This is the sense of his famous line that he had to deny knowledge to make room for faith, and it opens the way to his moral philosophy.

**A twist in the analogy.** Copernicus moved humanity _away_ from the centre of the universe. Kant seems to put the knowing subject _at_ the centre of the known world. What the two share is the method: explain what appears by the standpoint and activity of the observer, rather than by the object alone.

## Cognitive science
I agree, and the title fits. Many of cognitive science’s central ideas are Kantian, whether or not the people working with them have read him.

Some of the clearest lines of descent:

- **Experience as construction.** The core Kantian idea is that the mind doesn’t passively receive the world; it builds a coherent experience out of raw input using its own structures. Helmholtz, who called perception “unconscious inference,” was steeped in neo-Kantianism. Today’s predictive processing models (Friston, Clark, Hohwy) are often described as Kant with probability theory: the brain predicts top-down, and the senses correct the predictions.
- **Synthesis and the binding problem.** Kant asked how scattered sensations become one object experienced by one subject. Neuroscience asks the same question when it studies how color, shape, and motion, processed in different areas, end up bound into a single perceived thing.
- **Schemata.** Kant’s schemata link abstract concepts to sensory images. They resurface in Bartlett’s schemas in memory research, in Piaget, and in cognitive psychology generally.
- **Innate structure.** Chomsky’s universal grammar and Spelke’s “core knowledge” (infants’ built-in expectations about objects, space, number, and agents) read almost like an empirical list of categories.

There’s also a nice tension here, which aspiring philosophers can learn from. Kant would have objected to much of this. For him, the forms and categories are _transcendental_: conditions of possible experience, not facts about brains to be found by experiment. Cognitive science naturalises them. Konrad Lorenz put it well: what is a priori for the individual is a posteriori for the species, built in by evolution. That solves some problems and creates others. If our categories are evolved adaptations, what grounds the necessity Kant cared about, and how do we know they track reality rather than just usefulness? This is where Kant still pushes back on his heirs, and why reading him directly rather than through the science is worthwhile.
